Slide 01

Sensitivity & specificity

Sensitivity and specificity describe two aspects of the same decision. Sensitivity tells us how successfully a system finds cases where the target is present. Specificity tells us how successfully it recognizes cases where the target is absent. A useful evaluation considers both, because a system can improve one simply by making more of one kind of prediction.

For example, a spam filter could catch every spam email by flagging every email. It would have perfect sensitivity, but it would also flag every legitimate message, giving it zero specificity. The important question is how well it distinguishes the classes.

The slides begin with a machine learning classifier, then apply the same logic to EEG and sensory experiments. In sensory neuroscience, “sensitivity” can also refer to d′, a different measure that we introduce later. All numerical examples are illustrative except the identified published study.

Slide 02

A classifier makes a decision

A binary classifier assigns each input to one of two classes. Here, the target class is spam, so spam is “positive” and legitimate email is “negative.” These words identify the classes rather than whether an outcome is desirable. In EEG classification, seizure activity can be the positive class instead.

Many models first generate a continuous score. A threshold converts that score into a decision: scores at or above the threshold count as positive. For example, a score of 0.7 would trigger a positive decision at a threshold of 0.5 but a negative decision at a threshold of 0.8. A model score need not be a calibrated probability.

Our test set contains 100 spam emails and 900 legitimate emails, giving a spam prevalence of 10%. We need reference labels to evaluate the predictions. In an actual experiment, choose the threshold using validation data, then evaluate it on separate test data.

Slide 03

The confusion matrix

The confusion matrix cross-tabulates the reference label and the model's prediction. In this deck, columns show the actual class and rows show the predicted class. Check those labels whenever you read a matrix, because other presentations may reverse the orientation.

A true positive is spam correctly flagged as spam: 90 emails. A false negative is spam that the filter misses: 10 emails. A false positive is a legitimate email incorrectly flagged as spam: 45 emails. A true negative is a legitimate email correctly accepted: 855 emails. The four cells add up to 1,000.

“Positive” or “negative” describes the prediction, while “true” or “false” tells you whether it matches the reference. The two error types have different consequences. Missing spam puts an unwanted message in the inbox. A false alarm can hide an important legitimate message. The matrix makes those consequences visible instead of combining them into one accuracy score.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics.

Slide 04

Sensitivity: actual positives

To calculate sensitivity, restrict attention to the emails that really are spam. There are 100: 90 true positives plus 10 false negatives. The filter catches 90 of them, so sensitivity = TP / (TP + FN) = 90 / 100 = 90%.

The denominator is the key. We are asking what fraction of actual positives received a positive prediction. Legitimate emails do not enter this calculation. In machine learning, recall and true positive rate are other names for this same quantity.

The remaining 10% of actual positives were missed, so the false negative rate is 1 − sensitivity = 10%. Higher sensitivity means fewer missed positives at the evaluated operating point. It does not establish that positive predictions are trustworthy, because this calculation ignores false positives. We need specificity and precision to understand that other side of performance.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics.

Slide 05

Specificity: actual negatives

To calculate specificity, restrict attention to legitimate emails. There are 900: 855 true negatives plus 45 false positives. The filter correctly leaves 855 alone, so specificity = TN / (TN + FP) = 855 / 900 = 95%.

The complementary quantity is the false positive rate. Here, 45 / 900 = 5% of actual negatives trigger a positive prediction. Therefore, false positive rate = 1 − specificity. This 5% is a proportion of legitimate emails, not a proportion of flagged emails.

Specificity and sensitivity do not add up to 100%. They describe different groups, so both can be high or both can be low. In our example, they are 95% and 90%. A perfect classifier would reach 100% on both. A classifier that always predicts negative would have 100% specificity and 0% sensitivity, assuming both classes are present.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics.

Slide 06

Move the decision threshold

This slide introduces a new simulated score model rather than reconstructing the exact email counts. The orange curve describes scores among actual negatives, and the teal curve describes scores among actual positives. Each curve has a total area of one. Their relative heights do not show how common the two classes are.

The dashed line is the decision threshold. Everything to its right receives a positive prediction. The area under the positive-class curve to the right is sensitivity. The area under the negative-class curve to the left is specificity. Where the curves overlap, some errors are unavoidable for this threshold rule.

Move the threshold left. More actual positives cross it, increasing sensitivity, but more actual negatives cross it too, reducing specificity. Move it right to reverse the effect. At the initial threshold of 1.00, both rates are about 84.1%. The distributions stay fixed while you move the slider: you are changing the decision policy, not improving the model's ability to separate the classes.

Reference: scikit-learn: ROC curve.

Slide 07

The ROC curve

A receiver operating characteristic, or ROC, curve summarizes how a classifier behaves across thresholds. Each threshold produces a sensitivity and a false positive rate. The ROC plots sensitivity vertically and false positive rate horizontally. Since false positive rate equals 1 − specificity, moving left means better specificity.

The upper-left corner represents perfect performance: every positive detected and no negatives falsely flagged. The diagonal represents equal positive-response probabilities in the two classes. At the extreme upper-right corner, everything is classified positive; at the lower-left corner, everything is classified negative.

The marker shows the threshold selected on the preceding slide. AUC, the area under the curve, summarizes ranking across thresholds, but it does not choose the operating point. That choice depends on the consequences of errors and the setting in which the classifier will be used. A favorable ROC curve can still accompany low precision when positives are rare, as the next examples show.

Reference: scikit-learn: ROC curve.

Slide 08

Sensitivity is different from precision

Sensitivity starts with actual positives. Precision starts with positive predictions. That change in denominator makes them answer different questions, even though both formulas contain true positives in the numerator.

In the email example, sensitivity is 90 / (90 + 10) = 90%: of the 100 actual spam emails, 90 were flagged. Precision is 90 / (90 + 45) ≈ 66.7%: of the 135 flagged emails, only 90 were spam. The other 45 were legitimate messages. Thus, catching most spam does not imply that every flagged message is probably spam to the same degree.

Specificity answers a third question: of the 900 legitimate emails, how many were correctly accepted? Its value is 95%. One useful habit is to state the denominator in words before calculating anything: actual positives for sensitivity, actual negatives for specificity, and positive predictions for precision. Precision is also called positive predictive value, or PPV.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics.

Slide 09

Rare events change precision

This comparison holds sensitivity and specificity fixed while changing how common the target is. At 10% prevalence in 10,000 cases, there are 1,000 actual positives. A 90% sensitivity detects 900. There are 9,000 negatives, and a 5% false positive rate creates 450 false alerts. Precision is therefore 900 / (900 + 450) ≈ 66.7%.

At 1% prevalence, there are only 100 actual positives, giving 90 true alerts. But there are now 9,900 negatives, giving 495 false alerts. Precision falls to 90 / (90 + 495) ≈ 15.4%. The classifier's conditional detection rates are unchanged, yet most alerts now come from negative cases.

This matters whenever a system monitors for uncommon events, including seizure activity in long recordings. The calculation assumes a pure prevalence change. Real changes in participants, recording quality, or case difficulty can also alter sensitivity and specificity. You should not assume those rates remain fixed across every new population.

Slide 10

EEG seizure classification

The classification logic transfers directly to EEG when we define an evaluation unit. Here, each unit is a fixed recording window, and experts label whether that window contains seizure activity. The model then predicts seizure or non-seizure for each window.

Among 20 actual seizure windows, it flags 18 and misses two. Sensitivity is 18 / 20 = 90%. Among 980 non-seizure windows, it correctly rejects 931 and falsely flags 49. Specificity is 931 / 980 = 95%. There are 67 flagged windows in total, so precision is 18 / 67 ≈ 26.9%.

Despite high sensitivity and specificity, most flagged windows are false positives in this constructed example. Another issue is that one seizure may occupy several windows. Detecting 90% of seizure windows is not necessarily the same as detecting 90% of seizures. To interpret these numbers, we need the window duration, labeling rule, and reference standard. The next slide changes the unit from windows to whole events.

Slide 11

Windows, events and alarms

Window-based and event-based analyses count different things. A long seizure may contribute many positive windows, while a short seizure contributes only a few. An event-based analysis gives each reference seizure one opportunity to be counted as detected, according to a specified matching rule.

For a simple illustration, imagine one seizure occupies ten windows and a second occupies one. A detector that catches all ten windows of the first but misses the second has 10 / 11 ≈ 90.9% window sensitivity, but only 1 / 2 = 50% event sensitivity. The same predictions produce different percentages because the denominators changed.

False alarms per hour measures unmatched alarm events divided by monitoring time. It has units of alarms/hour. By contrast, 1 − specificity is a dimensionless fraction of actual negative units. They cannot be equated without additional definitions. Alarm merging and event matching also matter. Reus et al.'s published evaluation similarly illustrates how record-level and seizure-level definitions yield different sensitivities; those study results are not universal device specifications.

Reference: Reus et al. (2022).

Slide 12

Sensory detection experiments

In a tone-detection experiment, the experimenter knows whether a tone was present. The participant reports “yes” or “no.” This produces exactly the same four categories as a classifier, but psychophysics uses the terms hit, miss, false alarm, and correct rejection.

In the example, the participant says “yes” on 80 of 100 tone trials, giving a hit rate of 80%. They also say “yes” on 20 of 100 noise-only trials, giving a false alarm probability of 20% and a correct rejection rate of 80%. These correspond to classification sensitivity, false positive rate, and specificity, respectively.

Now imagine encouraging the participant to report even a faint suspicion of a tone. They may produce more hits and more false alarms without any improvement in the underlying sensory evidence. A change in willingness to answer “yes” can therefore alter the hit rate. This motivates a model that distinguishes sensory discriminability from the response criterion.

Reference: Stanislaw & Todorov (1999).

Slide 13

Sensitivity can mean d′

The word “sensitivity” has two related meanings here. Classification sensitivity is the hit rate at a particular decision threshold. Signal detection theory often uses sensitivity to mean d′, pronounced “d-prime,” which describes discriminability under a model of internal evidence.

For equal-variance Gaussian signal and noise distributions, d′ = z(H) − z(F), where H is hit rate, F is false alarm probability, and z converts a probability to a standard normal quantile. In this model, d′ measures the separation between the two means in units of their common standard deviation.

With H = 0.80 and F = 0.20, z(H) ≈ 0.842 and z(F) ≈ −0.842. Their difference is about 1.68. This is not 168%: d′ is a separation measure, not a percentage. It can remain unchanged when the observer changes criterion. That interpretation relies on the model assumptions. Exact rates of zero or one require special treatment before applying the inverse-normal formula to finite-sample data.

Reference: Stanislaw & Todorov (1999).

Slide 14

Evidence versus decision criterion

The two sliders manipulate different parts of the signal detection model. Discriminability d′ controls the separation of the evidence distributions. Criterion c controls where the observer places the decision boundary relative to their midpoint. Positive c means a more conservative rule, while negative c means a more liberal rule.

First keep d′ at 1.68 and move c left. Hit rate rises, but false alarm probability rises too. The distributions have not moved apart, so the observer has not gained discriminability. They are simply more willing to answer “yes.” Moving c right produces fewer hits and fewer false alarms.

Then set c to zero and increase d′. The distributions separate, increasing both sensitivity and specificity. At d′ = 0, the distributions coincide. With c = 0, hits and false alarms are both 50%. More generally, identical distributions produce equal hit and false alarm probabilities at any shared threshold. This demonstrates why a high hit rate alone cannot establish good sensory discrimination.

Reference: Stanislaw & Todorov (1999).

Slide 15

Neural decoding and selectivity

A neural decoder uses measured neural activity to predict an external label, such as whether a particular stimulus was present. Once we define the target, reference labels, and prediction rule, we can compute sensitivity and specificity just as we did for emails or EEG windows.

A neuron can also be described as selective for a stimulus because its response differs across stimulus conditions. This use of “selective” or “specific” does not automatically define a binary classification metric. We would need a rule such as “predict the target whenever the firing rate exceeds this threshold,” plus labeled target and non-target trials, to construct a confusion matrix.

For an illustrative decoder, firing on 90 of 100 target trials gives 90% sensitivity. If it also fires above threshold on 40 of 100 non-target trials, specificity is only 60%. A strong target response is therefore insufficient to establish reliable detection. Successful decoding also does not, by itself, establish that the recorded neuron or brain region is uniquely or causally responsible for the stimulus-related process.

Slide 16

Comparison at a glance

This slide collects the definitions so you can choose a measure by the question it answers. Sensitivity evaluates actual positives and counts how many you detected. Specificity evaluates actual negatives and counts how many you correctly rejected. Precision evaluates positive predictions and counts how many were correct.

False positive rate is the complement of specificity because both divide by the same actual-negative total. False negative rate is the complement of sensitivity because both divide by the actual-positive total. Sensitivity and specificity themselves are not complements. d′ answers a model-based question about discriminability using both hits and false alarms.

When reporting results, attach each rate to its positive class, evaluation unit, threshold, and reference labels. Include the counts so readers can see the denominator. For example, 9 / 10 and 900 / 1,000 both equal 90%, but carry different sampling uncertainty. A rate is undefined when its denominator is zero. Repeated windows or trials from one participant may be dependent, which matters when estimating uncertainty.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics, Stanislaw & Todorov (1999).

Slide 17

Check your understanding

The detector correctly flags 45 of 50 positive windows. Therefore TP = 45 and FN = 5. Sensitivity is 45 / (45 + 5) = 90%. The detector falsely flags 20 of 950 negative windows, so FP = 20 and TN = 930. Specificity is 930 / (930 + 20) ≈ 97.9%.

The first answer choice confuses specificity with precision. Precision is 45 / (45 + 20) ≈ 69.2%, because there are 65 flagged windows altogether. The third choice swaps sensitivity and specificity. Writing down the four counts before computing rates helps prevent both mistakes.

For the bonus question, a participant who says “yes” on every trial hits every signal but also falsely reports every noise-only trial. Classification sensitivity is 100% and specificity is 0%. The responses do not demonstrate discrimination between signal and noise. Directly substituting hit and false alarm rates of one into the d′ formula is undefined, so an empirical d′ estimate would require an appropriate correction or model.

Slide 18

References & further reading

The references serve different purposes. The scikit-learn evaluation documentation defines classification metrics, while its ROC documentation explains threshold-dependent true positive and false positive rates. Stanislaw and Todorov provide a methodological treatment of signal detection measures. Reus and colleagues supply the real EEG example about different evaluation units.

The deck's other numbers and distributions are teaching examples, not measured performance claims. When comparing real studies, check whether they evaluate the same target class, population, unit of analysis, and reference standard. An event sensitivity and a window sensitivity cannot be treated as directly interchangeable simply because both carry the name “sensitivity.”

The central habit is to ask what appears in the denominator. Actual positives identify sensitivity, actual negatives identify specificity, and positive predictions identify precision. When neuroscience uses the word “sensitivity,” also check whether it means hit rate at a criterion or model-based discriminability such as d′.

Reference: scikit-learn: classification metrics, scikit-learn: ROC curve, Stanislaw & Todorov (1999), Reus et al. (2022).
