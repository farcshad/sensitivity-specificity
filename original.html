Sensitivity is P(predicted positive given actual positive). It asks about the population of actual positives, not the population of positive predictions. A classifier that marks every email as spam has 100% sensitivity, but 0% specificity when both classes are present.

AUC summarizes ranking across thresholds. It does not choose a threshold or tell you how many alerts will be correct.

Reference: scikit-learn, ROC curve. Curve uses the illustrative model from slide 6.

The upper-left corner combines high sensitivity with low false positive rate. The diagonal is the no-discrimination reference. AUC can remain unchanged while a change in prevalence alters precision. On a finite test set the empirical ROC is a staircase, unlike this smooth population curve.

Back to the email example

Sensitivity and precision ask different questions

Sensitivity

Among actual spam, what fraction did we flag?

90 / (90 + 10)
90%

Precision

Among flagged emails, what fraction really are spam?

90 / (90 + 45)
66.7%

Precision uses TP + FP. Specificity uses TN + FP.

Precision is also called positive predictive value (PPV). Definition reference: scikit-learn, classification metrics.

Accuracy here is (90 + 855)/1000 = 94.5%. An always-negative model would achieve 90% accuracy on this imbalanced test set while detecting no spam. Sensitivity and specificity expose this failure. Balanced accuracy is the mean of sensitivity and specificity in a binary task.

Prevalence = fraction of actual positives

Rare events make false alerts matter

Hold sensitivity at 90% and specificity at 95%.

At 1% prevalence, most alerts are false even though sensitivity and specificity look high.

Illustrative calculation. A change only in prevalence leaves these conditional rates fixed. Real population shifts can also change sensitivity and specificity.

PPV = sensitivity × prevalence / [sensitivity × prevalence + (1 − specificity) × (1 − prevalence)]. This is Bayes' rule. The second scenario has 90 true alerts and 495 false alerts. Do not assume fixed sensitivity and specificity when disease severity, noise, hardware, or other class-conditional characteristics change.

A miss leaves a seizure window unflagged. A false positive flags a non-seizure window, potentially because of an artifact or another EEG pattern.

These are teaching counts, not a device performance claim. A window label and a whole-seizure detection are different evaluation units.

Define the window duration, overlap, seizure-label rule, and handling of ambiguous expert annotations before computing a matrix. Correlated windows from a patient are not independent observations. The real-study source on the next slide illustrates why the unit of analysis matters.

The unit of analysis matters

Detecting windows versus detecting seizures

Window-based evaluation

Each fixed window has a positive or negative label.

Sensitivity = positive windows detected / all positive windows

Specificity = negative windows rejected / all negative windows

Event-based evaluation

Each seizure counts as one event, with a defined matching rule.

Sensitivity = seizures detected / all reference seizures

False alarms per hour = unmatched alarms / monitoring hours

False alarms per hour is a frequency. It is not 1 − specificity.

Published example: Reus et al. (2022) reported 98% sensitivity at record level and 93% at seizure level for Persyst in their evaluation. The definition of a positive changed.

Reus et al., Automated seizure detection in an EMU setting. Results apply to that study's monitoring and evaluation protocol.

At record level, detecting at least one seizure can make the whole record positive. At seizure level, every seizure is evaluated separately. In event detection there is no unique count of negative events, so sensitivity, false alarms per time, and detection delay are often more interpretable than a specificity inferred from arbitrary windows. Alarm merging and matching tolerances must be documented.

Terminology: Stanislaw & Todorov (1999).

In a yes/no sensory experiment, a known external stimulus provides the reference label. The decision may reflect internal sensory evidence as well as expectations and response incentives. The four outcome categories correspond exactly to the confusion matrix.

A terminology trap

In signal detection theory, sensitivity often means d′

Classification sensitivity

TP / (TP + FN)

The hit rate at one decision criterion.

It changes when the observer becomes more willing to say “yes.”

Signal detection sensitivity

d′ = z(H) − z(F)

The separation of signal and noise under a specified model.

H = hit rate, F = false alarm probability.
z = inverse standard normal CDF.

For H = 0.80 and F = 0.20:
d′ = 0.842 − (−0.842) ≈ 1.68.

Under the equal-variance Gaussian model, d′ separates discriminability from response criterion. Its interpretation relies on the model assumptions.

Stanislaw & Todorov (1999), calculation of signal detection measures.

In this model, d′ equals the difference between the signal and noise means divided by their common standard deviation. It is not a percentage. Raw rates of 0 or 1 yield infinite z scores. For finite samples, use a justified correction or model-based estimate and report the method. Unequal variances and other model violations complicate a criterion-independent interpretation.

Illustrative equal-variance Gaussian model. Noise mean = 0, signal mean = d′, SD = 1. Threshold = d′ / 2 + c.

c = −[z(H) + z(F)]/2. Setting d′ to 0 makes the two distributions identical. A liberal criterion can then produce many hits, but it produces equally many false alarms. Raising d′ at a fixed midpoint-relative criterion increases both sensitivity and specificity. This illustrates why hit rate alone cannot isolate perceptual ability.

Interpreting brain responses

Neural decoding and neural selectivity

A decoder predicts a stimulus

A model uses neural activity to predict whether a target stimulus was present.

Sensitivity and specificity compare its predictions with the stimulus labels.

For several stimulus classes, define one class as positive and the rest as negative, then report results for each class.

A neuron responds selectively

A neuron may respond more strongly to one stimulus than to alternatives.

The word “specific” here describes tuning or selectivity. It does not automatically mean TN / (TN + FP).

A confusion matrix requires an explicit binary target and a response threshold.

A strong response to the target is insufficient. Responses to non-targets determine false positives.

This is a conceptual example, not a claim about a particular brain region. A firing-rate threshold could classify trials as target present or absent. However, decoding performance alone does not establish that a neuron or region is uniquely responsible for a cognitive function. Define whether the goal is generalization to new trials, sessions, or people, and choose held-out data accordingly.

04 / Review

The denominator tells you the question

MeasureCalculationQuestion
SensitivityTP / (TP + FN)How many actual positives did we detect?
SpecificityTN / (TN + FP)How many actual negatives did we reject?
Precision / PPVTP / (TP + FP)How many positive predictions were correct?
False positive rateFP / (FP + TN)How often did actual negatives trigger a positive?
d′z(H) − z(F)How separated are signal and noise in the SDT model?

Sensitivity concerns actual positives. Specificity concerns actual negatives.

For any report, specify the positive class, evaluation unit, threshold, and reference labels. Include counts and uncertainty. Zero denominators make the corresponding rate undefined.

Include confidence intervals in empirical work, with an approach that respects dependence among trials or windows from the same participant. In machine learning, select thresholds on validation data and evaluate the chosen threshold on held-out data. A favorable metric cannot repair unreliable reference labels or leakage between training and evaluation.

Bonus question

What if the participant in a tone experiment says “yes” on every trial?

Reveal the answer

Sensitivity = 100%.
Specificity = 0%.
Hit rate alone is misleading.

Answer: sensitivity = 45 / 50 = 90%. Specificity = 930 / 950 ≈ 97.9%. Saying “yes” on every trial gives 100% sensitivity and 0% specificity.

The false positive count is 20, so TN = 950 − 20 = 930. Precision is 45/(45 + 20) ≈ 69.2%, the distractor in the first choice. For the bonus, raw hit and false alarm rates are both 1, so direct d′ calculation needs an extreme-rate correction or model-based treatment. The behavioral strategy itself does not demonstrate sensory discrimination.
